How to Brush a Pomeranian Puppy That Thinks the Brush Is a Toy: A Week-by-Week Routine

Owner-tested ways to calm a mouthy Pom at grooming time, a six-week practice plan and the tools that suit a puppy coat.

If you want to know how to brush a Pomeranian puppy that thinks the brush is a toy, start by changing the goal. For the first weeks you are not grooming a coat, you are teaching a tiny, mouthy dog that the brush is boring, predictable and worth standing still for. The puppy fluff barely needs the work yet. The habit does, because the adult double coat that follows will need brushing for the rest of your dog's life.

Quick answer: We read 40 owner replies in three r/Pomeranians threads about puppies that fight or play with the brush. 25 of the 40 (63%) relied on food: treats fed during brushing, a lick mat, or a long-lasting chew. The next most common fixes were short daily sessions (18%) and brushing a sleepy puppy (15%). Combine them: let the puppy investigate the brush, pay for calm, stop before the grab, and build from a few strokes to a full-body session over about six weeks.

Why does a Pomeranian puppy treat the brush as a toy?

Look at the brush from the puppy's side: it has a handle that fits a small mouth, it moves quickly near the face, and it arrives while a favourite person is leaning over, talking and touching. Everything about it says game. A puppy that pounces, grabs and shakes the brush is not being stubborn. It is doing what puppies do with moving objects.

That is good news. A playful puppy is far easier to teach than a frightened one, because you only have to make calm more rewarding than the game. One owner in the main thread passed on what their trainer had said: the early sessions are "just for the act of brushing and getting used to it, rather than it being useful for the coat", and patience tends to grow along with the coat.

Play or stress? Read the body first

Before you train, check which problem you have. A wiggly body, a wagging tail and bouncy grabs at the brush are play. Stress looks different. Dr. Aly Cohen, an extension veterinarian at Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center who shares her home with a Pomeranian, lists the early stress signs to watch for during grooming as lip licking, yawning when not tired, freezing, pulling away and showing the whites of the eyes in her February 2026 note on cooperative grooming.

What the tally shows

Food wins by a wide margin, but notice how owners use it. Almost nobody bribes a puppy that is already biting. They give the mouth a job before the brush moves: a lick mat, a chew held in the paws, or a steady drip of tiny treats. One owner brushes "until he is done with his chew", then stops. Another started with a lick mat on the floor, moved to a recliner, and now needs no mat at all, only a treat at the end.

The second pattern is timing. Six owners brush a sleepy puppy, often one that has dozed off in a lap, and several say their adult dogs now fall asleep during grooming. Two more tire the puppy out first. If your Pom's daily exercise is still guesswork, our comparison of how long owners walk their Pomeranians gives you a sensible range to work with.

What this tally can't tell you

These are self-selected replies from one online community, sorted by hand, and nobody reported back months later with a result. Treat the percentages as "what owners reach for first", not as success rates. They are useful because they line up with what the professional sources below recommend.

What a veterinarian and a behaviour handout add

Cornell's Dr. Cohen describes the approach as cooperative care: teaching a dog to take part in handling through gradual exposure at the dog's pace, paired with positive reinforcement. She writes that these skills are best learned in puppyhood, and suggests pairing grooming tools with treats before ever using them and letting the dog investigate brushes at its own pace. Sessions should be short and predictable and end on a positive note, and "stopping early while things are still going well is a success".

A desensitisation handout from Langford Vets in the UK breaks brushing into four separate things a dog has to accept: seeing the brush, the brush moving toward the dog, the brush touching the dog, and the brush moving through the coat. It was written for anxious dogs, which is a harder case than a playful puppy, but the four steps are exactly the ladder a mouthy Pom needs to climb. The handout also recommends some exercise before a session, holding the session before a meal so food matters more, and using lick mats spread with wet food. It warns that you may only be able to bring the brush 0.5 cm closer at a time, and tells owners not to rush.

Put the two together with the owner tally and the picture is consistent: food first, tiny steps, short sessions, stop early.

The week-by-week brushing routine

This six-week routine is our own arrangement of those four steps, using the tactics owners named most often. The session lengths are starting suggestions, not measured figures, so let the planner above and your puppy's body language set the real pace. If a week goes badly, repeat it. Two or three tiny sessions a day beat one long one.

Week 1: make the brush boring

  1. Put the brush on the floor during a calm moment and let your puppy sniff it. Feed a small treat for sniffing, and another for looking away from it.
  2. If the puppy picks it up, do not chase or tug. Trade it for a treat and put it back down. One owner left the brush on the floor for a few days and found the puppy stopped reacting to it.
  3. Pick the brush up, feed a treat, put it down. Repeat until picking it up no longer starts a game.
  4. No brushing yet. Handle paws, ears and tail gently for a second each while the puppy is relaxed, as Cornell suggests.

Week 2: touch without brushing

  1. Set up a lick mat or hold a chew, so the mouth is busy before the brush moves.
  2. Rest the smooth back of the brush on the shoulder for one second, then take it away and feed.
  3. Turn the brush over and rest the pins or bristles on the coat without moving them. Feed.
  4. If teeth land on the brush, freeze it against your body, wait for the puppy to let go, and reward the first calm second. One owner taught "gently" this way, paying each time the puppy was not biting the brush.

Week 3: first real strokes

  1. Start at the back of the neck and shoulders, which one owner pointed out many dogs find relaxing.
  2. Make three to five short, light strokes in the direction the coat grows, then stop and let the puppy finish the lick mat.
  3. End the session before the usual grab. If your puppy lasts ten seconds, stop at six or seven.
  4. Try one session with a puppy that is dozing in your lap, the tactic six owners swore by.

Week 4: cover more coat

  1. Extend the strokes along the back, down the sides and across the chest.
  2. Mist the coat lightly with a detangling or conditioning spray before you brush. One owner noticed that rubbing the spray in also marks the switch from play time to grooming time.
  3. Introduce a comb for a few strokes so a second tool becomes normal.
  4. Give the session a fixed place. Two owners found that a table with a non-slip mat worked where the lap and the couch, which the puppy sees as play territory, did not. Never leave a puppy unattended on a raised surface.

Week 5: the tricky zones

  1. Take one zone per session: behind the ears, the trousers, the tail or the legs.
  2. Go back to week 3 rules for each new zone: a few strokes, then stop.
  3. If you meet a small tangle, tease it apart with your fingers before any tool touches it. One owner describes pulling a mat open with thumbs and forefingers "like a hamburger bun", because a brush dragging on a mat hurts.
  4. Behind the ears is the spot owners in the threads keep mentioning for mats, so make it a daily two-second touch even on non-brushing days.

Week 6: the first full session

  1. Work in the same order every time so the session is predictable: neck, back, sides, chest, trousers, tail, ears.
  2. Fade the lick mat. Feed a treat after each body section, then only at the end.
  3. Take a break if the puppy asks for one and finish later in the day, as one owner does, rather than holding the puppy down to finish.
  4. From here you are ready for an adult rhythm. Our Pomeranian grooming schedule comparison shows how often owners brush, bathe and book a groomer once the adult coat is in.

The same ladder of look, touch, wear and move works for other equipment a puppy objects to. If yours also wrestles the harness, the gentle harness training plan follows the same logic.

Brush guide: which tool for which week?

Five of the 40 owners said the turning point was a gentler tool, not a training trick. One was blunt: with a three-month-old that hated brushing, they advised dropping the firm slicker, starting with a comb and a soft bristle brush, and keeping sessions to a couple of strokes. Another owner did use a slicker, but only after a few days of light brushing with a softer brush. An experienced owner in the same threads relies on a stainless steel comb, a fine comb for small tangles behind the ears and a pin brush whose pins do not scratch.

A simple test from one owner: brush the back of your own hand in front of the puppy. It shows the dog the tool is harmless, and it tells you in one stroke whether the pins are too sharp.

Using food without overfeeding a tiny dog

A Pomeranian puppy does not have much room for extras, and a week of brushing practice can add up. Owners in the threads handled it three ways: one-calorie training treats, a thin smear on a lick mat instead of a pile of biscuits, and a chew that lasts the whole session. One owner filled a rubber toy with frozen peanut butter and has been reducing the amount as the puppy improves. If you use peanut butter, another owner's reminder applies: check the label and avoid any product with xylitol.

Take the treats out of the daily ration instead of adding them on top, and break each one into crumbs. Our Pomeranian feeding amount guide shows what owners feed by weight, which makes it easier to see how small a brushing reward should be.

Common mistakes that turn brushing into a game

  • Pulling the brush away fast. A brush that jerks back is prey. Hold it still against your body until the puppy lets go.
  • Brushing a wide-awake puppy on the play couch. Owners who moved to a mat on a table, or waited for a sleepy moment, reported the fighting stopped.
  • Feeding after the bite. Give the mouth its job before the first stroke.
  • Going for a full groom in week 1. In the Cornell note, pushing past a dog's comfort level is what makes the next session harder.
  • Brushing through a tangle. One painful pull can undo a week of practice.
  • Stopping only when the puppy protests. That teaches protest. Stop while things are going well.
  • Skipping days. The owners with the calmest adults describe a little brushing every day in puppyhood. Tie it to something you already do, the same way a fixed schedule helps in our potty training reset plan.

When does it get easier?

Nobody in the threads gave a firm date, and you should distrust anyone who does. What owners describe is a slow fade: one three-month-old was already better after about three weeks of practice, one owner spent "weeks and weeks" on treats and tiny sessions, and one cheerful owner admits their Pom is a year old and still tries to play, but gets brushed anyway with praise and treats. Several owners of adult dogs say their Poms now run to the lap when the brush appears or fall asleep halfway through.

Judge progress by three things you can count: seconds of calm brushing before the first grab, how many body zones you can reach, and how much food you still need. If all three are moving the right way over a fortnight, the routine is working. If your puppy shows the stress signs from the table above and small steps do not help, Cornell's advice is to ask your veterinarian or a qualified trainer who uses humane methods.

Frequently asked questions

How do I stop my Pomeranian puppy from biting the brush?

Give the mouth something else to do before the brush moves, such as a lick mat or a chew, keep sessions shorter than the time it normally takes your puppy to grab, and reward calm seconds. In 40 owner replies we read, 63% used food in one of these ways.

When should I start brushing a Pomeranian puppy?

As soon as the puppy is home. The top-voted answer in a 2026 owner thread on when to start grooming was that it is never too early to get a puppy used to brushing, a little each day. Cornell's canine health center also says these skills are best learned in puppyhood.

How long should a puppy brushing session be?

Shorter than your puppy's patience. Time how many seconds pass before the biting starts and stop at about two thirds of that. Owners describe sessions of only a couple of strokes at first, building toward 5 to 10 minutes.

What is the best brush for a Pomeranian puppy?

For the learning weeks, owners in the threads favoured gentle tools: a soft bristle brush or grooming glove, a flexible detangling brush, a smooth pin brush and a metal comb. One owner introduced a slicker only after a few days of gentle brushing, and another advised dropping a firm slicker altogether for a puppy that hated it.

Should I brush my puppy when it is asleep?

Brushing a sleepy or dozing puppy was the third most common tactic, named in 6 of 40 replies. Owners say it teaches that grooming is relaxing. Keep the strokes very light, and still practise short awake sessions so the puppy learns to accept the brush when alert.

Is it bad if my puppy thinks brushing is fun?

No. Play is a much better starting point than fear. The aim is not to end the fun but to make standing still and licking a mat more rewarding than grabbing the brush.

What if my puppy only hates being brushed in one place?

Check that spot for a tangle, especially behind the ears. Owners describe loosening small mats with their fingers before using any tool, because a brush pulling on a mat is painful and can sour the whole routine.

The calm brush is built, not found

Learning how to brush a Pomeranian puppy that treats the brush as a toy is mostly a matter of order: the brush becomes boring, then it touches, then it moves, then it covers the whole dog. Keep the mouth busy, keep the sessions short, choose a tool that does not pull, and stop while it is still going well. Six unhurried weeks now buy you years of easy grooming.
