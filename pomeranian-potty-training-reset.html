Pomeranian Still Not Potty Trained at 6–8 Months? Why Small Puppies Lag and How to Reset

A calm, step-by-step plan to move a pad-loving Pom outside, built on the research and on what trainers actually do.

If your Pomeranian is still not potty trained at 6–8 months, you are in much bigger company than the puppy forums make it look. Small dogs really do finish house-training later and less completely than big dogs, and a tiny bladder plus an indoor pad habit makes the last stretch the hardest. The good news: the fix is a short, structured reset, and you can absolutely run it yourself.

Quick answer: In a 2019 study of 735 dogs, only 67% of small dogs (20 lb or less) were fully house-trained, against 95% of large dogs. A 6–8 month Pom that still has accidents is not broken, it is usually under-scheduled. Shorten the gaps between outdoor trips, supervise or confine between trips, move the pad toward the door a foot or two a day, reward every outdoor success, and give the reset 2–4 weeks before you judge it.

Is it normal for a Pomeranian to still have accidents at 6–8 months?

Yes, it is common for small dogs, even if it is not the goal. A veterinary study found that one in three small dogs was not fully house-trained by their owners' own definition, so a Pom that is "mostly there" at 7 months sits right in the middle of the pack.

The numbers come from Amy Learn, Lisa Radosta and Amy Pike, who surveyed owners online and published the results in the Journal of Veterinary Behavior in 2019. As psychologist Stanley Coren summarized in his Psychology Today write-up of the study, the researchers compared dogs of 20 lb or less with dogs of 40 lb or more and dropped the in-between sizes to make the difference clearer.

What "fully house-trained" meant in the study

The bar was clear and fair: for at least the last two months, apart from unusual circumstances, the dog had consistently eliminated outside or in a place the owner chose. A pee pad or litter setup counted as an approved place. So the 33% were not dogs that used pads; they were dogs that still went in places their owners did not want.

What the AKC expects at six months

AKC Chief Veterinarian Dr. Jerry Klein puts it this way in the AKC puppy potty training timeline: if training begins early, a 6-month-old puppy can usually be depended on to eliminate outside "most of the time". Most of the time is not every time. If you see no progress at all, the AKC advises a vet visit to rule out a medical cause, and that is a sensible first step before any reset.

Why do small-breed puppies lag behind?

Nobody has a single proven answer, but the researchers offered two likely reasons: small dogs may need to go more often because of their size, and they more often live where the outdoors is not one quick step away.

Both fit a lot of Pom homes: apartments, pads in the first months, and accidents that are small. A Pom accident is a small puddle that is easy to miss. A missed accident is a successful rehearsal from the puppy's point of view: the dog went, felt relief and nothing told it that the rug was the wrong spot.

Training itself moves the needle

One finding in the study is genuinely encouraging. Small dogs that had more formal obedience training, from basic classes up to competition level, were more likely to be fully house-trained, even though those classes never teach potty habits directly. Structure, routine and a habit of listening to you seem to carry over. That means the work you put into a gentle routine, like the one in our harness training plan for Poms, is not separate from house-training. It all adds up.

The pad habit is a real habit

A pad teaches a dog that soft, absorbent material underfoot is the right place. That is why so many pad-raised dogs pick bath mats and rugs when the pad disappears. Preventive Vet warns against simply removing the pad overnight for this reason, because the dog will look for an alternative "pad" such as a bathroom mat or a hallway rug.

How long can a 6–8 month Pomeranian hold it?

Use the AKC month-plus-one rule as a ceiling, not a target: a puppy's age in months plus one gives the maximum number of hours it should be able to hold it comfortably. For a 7-month Pom that ceiling is 8 hours, but during a reset you want trips far more often than that.

The AKC's own example is a 3-month-old puppy, which should manage about 4 hours in a crate without a mess. The rule describes the most a puppy can hold when resting. An awake, playing Pom needs out much sooner, and the AKC lists the moments that matter most: first thing in the morning, 5 to 30 minutes after meals, after drinking, after naps and after play.

The suggested gaps are our planning numbers, not a study result. They are deliberately short so your Pom gets many chances to be right and very few chances to be wrong. You stretch them later, once the accident-free days pile up.

Meals shape the schedule

Fixed meal times give you predictable potty times. The AKC also suggests not feeding within 2 hours of bedtime. If you are not sure how much your Pom should eat at each meal, our guide to Pomeranian feeding amounts by weight helps you split the day into regular, measured meals instead of a bowl that is always half full.

Which pad-to-outdoor path should you choose?

Pick the path that matches your home. A gradual pad move suits most Pom households, a full switch works when you can supervise closely, and keeping one pad is a fair choice for high-rise living.

The gradual move comes from Preventive Vet's guide to stopping potty pad use: move the pad a foot or two closer to the door every day until it sits right by the door, then place it outside, then make it smaller. Their pro tip is the best accelerator you have: whenever you see your dog head for the pad, take the dog outside instead and reward there.

If you have pads in several rooms

Fold them into one spot first. Preventive Vet suggests moving the extra pads toward the main pad in small steps and then removing them, and simply gating off upstairs or downstairs pad areas with a baby gate or a closed door in a multi-story home.

Step by step: the 3-week pad-to-outdoor reset

This plan assumes your vet has seen no medical reason for the accidents. Write the start date on a calendar; seeing a streak build is a great motivator for both of you.

Week 1: tighten the routine

  1. Clean old spots properly. The AKC recommends sanitizing soiled areas with pet stain cleaning products so the dog is not drawn back to the same spot.
  2. Pick up rugs and bath mats for now, especially if the planner flagged soft-surface accidents.
  3. Set fixed trip times. Waking, after every meal, after drinking, after naps and play, and every 2 hours in between.
  4. Use one outdoor spot and one cue. Stand quietly, say your cue ("go potty") as your Pom starts, and praise and reward right after the dog finishes, as the AKC describes.
  5. Play for a minute after success. The AKC suggests playing with your puppy for a few minutes before rushing back inside, which keeps outdoor trips rewarding.
  6. Supervise or confine. When you cannot watch, use a crate or playpen, which Preventive Vet also recommends to prevent accidents during the switch.
  7. Start the pad move. One to two feet toward the door per day.

Week 2: move outside

  1. Once the pad sits by the door and your Pom uses it, put it just outside the door.
  2. Watch for door visits and open the door every time, then reward on the outdoor pad.
  3. Begin shrinking the pad by cutting it smaller every few days.
  4. Add a door bell if you like. The AKC suggests ringing it as you go out together, then praising when the puppy rings it alone.

Week 3: stretch the gaps

  1. After 5 accident-free days in a row, lengthen the awake gap by 30 minutes.
  2. Stay well under the month-plus-one ceiling.
  3. If an accident happens, go back to the last gap that worked for a few days. That is normal progress, not failure.

Walks are a natural potty opportunity too. Short, regular outings work better for a reset than one long weekend hike; our look at how long Pomeranian owners walk each day shows how other owners fit outings around work.

Common mistakes that keep Poms on the pad

  • Punishing accidents. The AKC says not to punish your puppy. If you catch your Pom mid-accident, make a gentle "uh-oh" sound, carry the dog out and praise the finish outside. Wikipedia's overview of housebreaking notes that a negative reaction can discourage the animal and delay the training's success.
  • Pulling all pads at once. The dog keeps the "soft surface" rule and just chooses a rug.
  • Freedom too early. A Pom with the run of the house and no supervision gets to practice hidden accidents behind the sofa.
  • Rewarding late. A treat back inside rewards coming inside, not going outside. Carry treats with you.
  • Rushing back in. The AKC recommends a few minutes of play outside after your puppy goes, before heading back in.
  • Stretching gaps on good days. One great day is not a habit. Wait for the 5-day streak.
  • Ignoring the coat. A long, fluffy rear can get soiled and uncomfortable. If that happens, our Pomeranian grooming schedule covers routine coat care owners use.

When should you expect results?

Expect fewer accidents within the first week or two of a tight schedule, and a much more reliable dog after several weeks. The study's own yardstick for "fully trained" was two months without problems, so measure your success over weeks, not days.

Track three numbers on your calendar: accidents per week, longest accident-free streak, and your current awake gap. If accidents fall while the gap grows, the reset is working, even with the odd setback. If nothing improves after a few consistent weeks, talk to your vet and consider a qualified trainer; the study suggests structured training helps small dogs more than you might expect.

Adult size doesn't change the plan

Poms range quite a bit in adult size, as our adult Pomeranian weight reports show. A 4 lb and a 9 lb Pom both count as small dogs in the study above, and both benefit from the same short-gap routine.

Frequently asked questions

Is it too late to potty train a 7-month-old Pomeranian?

No. Many small dogs are still finishing house-training at this age; a 2019 study found only 67% of small dogs were fully house-trained. A structured reset with short gaps, supervision and rewards works at 7 months and later.

How often should a 6-month-old Pomeranian go out?

The AKC month-plus-one rule puts the ceiling at about 7 hours at rest, but during a reset aim for roughly every 2 hours while awake, plus right after waking, meals, drinking, naps and play.

Should I stop using pee pads completely?

Not overnight. Preventive Vet advises moving the pad a foot or two closer to the door each day, then outside, then shrinking it, because removing it abruptly can send the dog to rugs and bath mats.

Why does my Pom pee on rugs?

A pad teaches that soft, absorbent material is the right place, and rugs feel similar. Pick up rugs and bath mats during the reset and clean old spots with a pet stain cleaner.

Do pads count as being house-trained?

In the 2019 study, yes: eliminating on a pad or litter setup the owner chose counted. Being house-trained means the dog goes only where you want, whether that is outside or one approved indoor spot.

When should I see a vet about accidents?

Before you start a reset if you are unsure, and any time there is no progress. The AKC suggests a vet check when house-training is not moving forward, to rule out a medical cause.

You can do this

A Pomeranian still not potty trained at 6–8 months is a common small-dog story with a clear ending. Shorten the gaps, supervise, move the pad toward the door in small steps, reward every outdoor success and stretch the schedule only after a streak. Give it a few steady weeks and you will very likely see your Pom choosing the door on their own.
