Pomeranian Broken Leg (Radius/Ulna Fracture): Cast vs Surgery, Recovery Timeline and Real Costs

What veterinary surgeons say about splints versus plates for a toy dog's forearm, how many weeks healing really takes, and how to read a fracture quote before you sign it. Free planner included.

Home›Blog›Pomeranian Broken Leg

Published: 2026-10-13 · Pomeranian Hub

A Pomeranian broken leg is almost always a front leg, and almost always the same spot: the thin lower forearm just above the wrist, where the radius and ulna are barely thicker than a pencil lead. It usually happens in a split second, a jump off the sofa or a slip out of someone's arms. This guide gives you, for free, everything we wish every owner had in the waiting room: the cast-versus-surgery evidence, a realistic recovery calendar, the line items a fair quote should contain, and a planner you can fill in tonight.

Quick answer (TL;DR)
  • Cast or splint alone usually fails in toy breeds. Toy dogs have a nonunion rate of 80 percent or more for radius and ulna fractures, mostly when they are treated with external coaptation (casts and splints).
  • Surgery with a small plate is the standard recommendation for a Pomeranian with both forearm bones broken.
  • Healing timeline: roughly 6 to 12 weeks for dogs under six months old, and 2 to 5 months for adult dogs. In one recent case series of tiny dogs, the bones knitted in an average of 53 days.
  • Cost: anywhere from a few thousand dollars at a general practice to $8,000–$10,000 at a specialty hospital when both front legs are broken. Rechecks and implant removal are often billed on top.

Why do Pomeranians break their front legs so easily?

Because the distal (lower) radius in toy breeds is extremely thin and has a poor blood supply, a short fall onto the front feet concentrates the whole impact on a bone a few millimeters wide. The breed's habit of leaping off furniture does the rest.

Board-certified surgeon Dr. Jessica McCarthy (University of Wisconsin) writes in Veterinary Practice News that toy breed dogs are predisposed to distal radius and ulna fractures, and that their poor healing is mainly put down to reduced vascular density and less branching of the vessels in the distal radius. Less blood flow means fewer cells arrive to build new bone, which is why these fractures behave so differently from the same break in a Labrador.

How small are we talking?

A 2026 retrospective study from a veterinary emergency hospital in Murcia, Spain, looked at simple distal radius and ulna fractures in dogs under 5 kg. The most common breed in the study was the Pomeranian (4 of 8 dogs). Mean body weight was 3.12 kg (0.9–4.8 kg), and the radius measured on average just 0.35 cm across at its narrowest point (Preprints.org, locking-plate study of 10 cases). The same paper notes that radius and ulna fractures make up about 18% of all canine fractures, with a fall being the most common cause.

Typical ways it happens

  • Jumping down from a sofa, bed, car seat or the top of the stairs
  • Wriggling out of a person's arms, especially a child's
  • Being stepped on, or having a door or chair land on the paw
  • Falling from a grooming table or a chair. In the Spanish study, one dog broke the same leg again 70 days after its implants were removed, after falling from a chair

Signs your Pomeranian's leg is broken (and what to do in the first hour)

The classic sign is a front leg held completely off the ground, often with swelling above the wrist, a bend that should not be there, or crying when the forearm is touched. Some Poms still try to toe-touch, so partial weight bearing does not rule out a fracture. Only an X-ray can confirm a break.

First-hour checklist

  1. Pick the dog up with both hands under the chest, not by the front legs, and keep the injured leg hanging free.
  2. Do not try to straighten or splint the leg yourself. A badly placed home splint can turn a closed fracture into an open one.
  3. Confine the dog in a small carrier or a laundry basket lined with a towel for the drive.
  4. Call ahead so the clinic can prepare pain relief and X-rays.
  5. Mention the fall height. Dr. McCarthy notes that falls from a height often come with injuries to the chest, abdomen or nervous system, and these need checking before anyone plans the bone repair.
  6. Bring food and a sugar source if your Pom is a puppy. Pain, stress and fasting before anesthesia can push a tiny puppy toward low blood sugar. Our guide to Pomeranian puppy hypoglycemia covers the warning signs.

Cast vs surgery: which is better for a Pomeranian radius/ulna fracture?

For a Pomeranian with both forearm bones broken, surgery (usually a small plate and screws) is the evidence-based choice. A cast or splint alone has a very high failure rate in toy breeds. A cast is cheaper on day one but often costs more by the end.

The guidance in the Veterinary Practice News article is blunt: any toy breed dog with a radius and ulna fracture should have surgical fixation, usually rigid internal fixation, and should not be treated with a cast or a splint. The reason is the nonunion figure quoted above. When the bone never knits, you are back at the clinic weeks later, now paying for the surgery you were trying to avoid, plus all the splint changes before it.

When a splint can still work

The same article describes an exception: young, healthy dogs with only the radius or only the ulna broken, minimally displaced and closed (no wound), "can do great with six to 12 weeks of splinting". This is a narrow group, so ask your vet directly whether your Pom's X-ray shows one bone or both.

Why plates are not risk-free either

Older plating methods had a well-known flaw. A plate that is much stiffer than the bone carries the load itself, so the bone underneath thins out. The Spanish authors describe this "stress shielding" as a possible cause of delayed union, nonunion and refracture after implant removal. In their 10 fractures, repaired with a deliberately less rigid locking-plate construct, every bone healed. There were three minor complications (two seromas and one incision problem), and no plate failed. That is encouraging, but it is a single-center series of 10 fractures, not a guarantee.

How long does a Pomeranian broken leg take to heal?

Plan for roughly 6 to 12 weeks if your Pom is under six months old, and 2 to 5 months if they are an adult, as long as the fracture is properly stabilized. Full return to jumping and stairs comes after your vet confirms bone union on X-ray, not when the limp stops.

These ranges come from the Veterinary Practice News article. The Spanish plating study gives harder numbers for tiny dogs: a mean time to radiographic union of 53 days (range 28–113). Skeletally immature dogs averaged 47.9 days and mature dogs 66 days (range 56–76). The mean age in that study was 6.6 months, the age when a Pomeranian is at its most acrobatic.

Why speed matters before surgery

Young puppies heal astonishingly fast, which cuts both ways. Dr. McCarthy notes that in puppies under about 10 weeks old, a bony callus can form within 10 days. If surgery is planned, it should not be delayed, or the bone may start healing in the wrong position. In growing dogs, a poorly aligned repair can also lead to an angular limb deformity as the leg keeps growing.

How much does Pomeranian broken leg surgery cost?

Expect anything from a few thousand dollars at a general practice to five figures at a specialty hospital. The final total depends heavily on how many rechecks, sedations and implant-removal procedures are included. Always ask for an itemized estimate.

Very few hospitals publish fracture prices. One that does is the Queen's Veterinary School Hospital at the University of Cambridge, which lists fixed prices including VAT on its orthopaedics service page:

US specialty estimates can be higher still. One fundraiser for Olive, a 7-month-old Pomeranian in Wisconsin who broke both front legs while playing, quotes a specialty-hospital estimate of $8,000–$10,000 for the surgery.

Where the money really goes

  • Emergency exam and pain relief on the day of the injury, often at an ER before you even see a surgeon
  • Pre-op X-rays, sometimes repeated at the surgical hospital
  • Surgery package: anesthesia, implants, hospital stay, medication to take home
  • Recheck X-rays: tiny dogs often need sedation for clear images
  • Implant removal, partial or full, which may be done in stages
  • Cone, crate or pen, rugs and ramps for home
  • Cast route only: every splint change is a visit, and puppies may need one weekly

What does recovery look like at home?

The weeks after surgery are mostly about stopping a cheerful little dog from undoing the repair: tight confinement, a cone, leash-only toilet trips and zero jumping until the bone is confirmed healed. The repair can fail if a Pom jumps, so rest is the treatment, not an add-on.

Daily recovery checklist

  1. Pen or crate just big enough to stand, turn and lie flat, on a non-slip surface.
  2. Bandage or splint check twice a day. VCA Animal Hospitals advises checking at least twice daily that it is clean, dry and neither too tight nor too loose, and looking for swollen or red toes and any foul smell.
  3. Keep it dry. Slip a bag or protective boot over the splint for every toilet trip in wet grass, and remove it indoors.
  4. Watch for sudden chewing. VCA says a dog that suddenly starts chewing a bandage it previously ignored needs a call to the vet right away, because it often signals pain or a pressure sore.
  5. Use a harness, not a collar, for toilet trips. It gives you control without neck pressure, which matters for a breed prone to tracheal collapse.
  6. Pain meds on the clock, not "when he seems sore". Poms hide pain well.
  7. Feed puppies small, frequent meals, even if they are sulky in the cone.
  8. Block every jump-off point: sofa, bed, stairs. Add ramps before restrictions end.

Mental enrichment while confined

A bored Pomeranian is a jumping Pomeranian. Snuffle mats, lick mats, food puzzles fed in the crate, short calm training sessions (nose targets, "look") and simply carrying the dog around the house at your chest all help them cope with weeks of rest.

Common mistakes owners make with a broken Pom leg

  • Choosing a cast purely on price for a two-bone fracture, then paying for surgery later anyway after a nonunion.
  • Waiting "a few days to see". In young puppies a callus can form within about 10 days, which makes later repair harder.
  • Letting the dog off restrictions when the limp disappears. Pain fades before bone strength returns, so only the recheck X-ray decides.
  • Treating implant removal as the finish line. The Spanish authors describe refracture after implant removal as a recognized complication of these fractures.
  • Skipping the second opinion. A surgeon who regularly operates on toy-breed forearms may use implants sized for a 3 kg dog. Dr. McCarthy warns that an oversized implant risks thinning the bone (osteopenia).
  • Accepting a one-line quote. Rechecks, sedation and implant removal are where totals grow.

Can a Pomeranian broken leg heal on its own?

Rarely in a useful way. A toy-breed forearm left without rigid stabilization often fails to knit or heals crooked, and it can leave a painful, unstable leg. Some owners who cannot afford a repair discuss amputation with their vet as a last resort.

Bones that heal out of alignment (malunion) or fuse the radius and ulna together (synostosis) can limit wrist movement for life. In growing puppies, a bad repair can also distort the leg as it grows. If cost is the barrier, tell your vet early. Many will discuss payment plans, a referral center with clearer pricing, or a teaching hospital, before a cheap cast becomes an expensive second surgery.

FAQ: Pomeranian broken leg

Should my Pomeranian get a cast or surgery for a broken leg?

For a fracture of both the radius and ulna, veterinary surgeons recommend surgery, usually a plate and screws. Toy breeds have a nonunion rate of 80 percent or more when these fractures are treated with external coaptation. A splint may work for a young dog with only one bone broken, minimally displaced and with no wound.

How long does a Pomeranian broken leg take to heal?

With proper stabilization, dogs under six months old usually heal in about 6 to 12 weeks, and adult dogs in 2 to 5 months. In a 2026 study of tiny dogs with locking plates, the mean time to union was 53 days, ranging from 28 to 113.

How much does surgery for a Pomeranian broken leg cost?

It varies widely by region and hospital. The University of Cambridge vet hospital lists £4,500 for a simple plate repair and £500 for each sedated recheck with X-rays. A US specialty-hospital estimate for a Pomeranian with both front legs broken was $8,000–$10,000.

When can my Pomeranian jump on the sofa again after a fracture?

Only after a recheck X-ray shows the bone has healed and your surgeon lifts restrictions, which is typically after at least 6 to 12 weeks. Even then, ramps or steps are worth keeping, because Pomeranians often break a leg jumping off furniture.

Do the plates need to be removed?

Not always, but it is common in toy dogs. In the 2026 Spanish study, 9 of 10 repaired fractures had full or partial implant removal after healing, often in stages. Your surgeon will decide based on the X-rays.

How do I care for my Pomeranian's splint at home?

VCA Animal Hospitals advises checking it at least twice a day, keeping it dry with a bag or boot outdoors, and calling the vet for swollen toes, a bad smell or sudden chewing. Growing puppies may need a splint change every week.

Summary

A Pomeranian broken leg in the lower forearm is a toy-breed problem with a toy-breed answer: fast X-rays, rigid surgical repair for most two-bone fractures, and weeks of disciplined rest. The cheapest option on day one, a cast, is the one most likely to fail in these thin, poorly supplied bones. Use the planner above to put dates on the calendar and gaps on the quote. Then take your list of questions to the vet, so you can make the decision with the full picture rather than in a panic.
